# figure-out: the seat pass

Before the read is named, look at what it implies from the seat of the person it is for, the way
that person will meet it. The proposal describes the states its author thought of; the person
meets the rest too. The pass finds the few places where what they meet costs them most.

## Who runs it

Run it in a fresh, isolated context, as the independent re-derivation in `SKILL.md` does, so it
does not inherit the proposal's own blind spots. Hand it the proposal, who it is for and their
moment, this assignment's authority and retained decisions, the product's location and how to
start it where one exists, the steps under *What it does* — including ranking and keeping only the
findings that change the read — the stances below, and any Taste entry or project rule that bears
on them. It reads, runs and reports; it changes nothing in the project. Where the host offers no
isolated context, run it inline and say in the read that the seat look was not independent.

## What it does

A **journey** is one path a person takes through the thing: from how they arrive to leaving with
what they came for, or failing to.

1. **Derive the journeys from this person and this feature**, never from a fixed list. Always
   include how they first arrive from outside (a shared link, a search, signed out, a new
   device), how they come back (after a break, after signing in, after an error), and what goes
   wrong — then the journeys only this feature has: its modes and their settings, several of the
   thing or none, the moment just before and just after it.
2. **Walk them on the running product** where it exists and can be started: start it and use it as
   the person would — a browser, the command line, the API — and say what you saw. Where it cannot
   be opened, walk the proposal one step at a time and say that is what you did. Where the work
   changes one part of a moment, walk the whole moment the person meets there, not only the part
   asked about.
3. **At each choice the person would notice** — what is shown, where it lives, how they reach it,
   what it asks of them, what it leaves out — put two or three concrete alternatives side by side,
   the proposal's own among them, and judge them against the stances below.
4. **Rank and cut.** Rank what you found by what each costs the person. Return only the findings
   that change the read, usually one to three, each naming the journey, what the person meets,
   and the change. Drop the rest, however true; a list of every possible improvement buries the
   one that matters. A finding that is a user's call goes to the user as options, the way
   `SKILL.md` puts a user's call about what a person sees.

## Default stances

Judge each choice against these. They are defaults: a ratified Taste entry or a rule the project
states overrides any of them, without asking. Where the user's live steering contradicts a stored
Taste entry, follow the clash rule in `TASTE.md` → *Curation — the section is a prompt*.

1. **Just in time.** Ask for each input, and offer each capability, at the point the person needs
   it — not earlier, and not somewhere else. At a gate such as sign-in, payment or a permission,
   ask only for the gate's own choice and what the gate cannot work without; what a later step
   can ask waits for that step.
2. **Continuity.** An interruption — signing in, an error, a pause, a retry — returns the person
   to where they were, with their work kept.
3. **A coherent model.** States that cannot make sense together cannot occur, and the model holds
   everything the person can actually have: several of a thing, none, each mode with its own
   settings applied.
4. **Familiar.** Follow the conventions the product and its people already use before inventing a
   new one.
5. **Respect the moment.** Add no request, prompt or sale where the person is committed, stressed
   or in the middle of a task.
6. **Value first.** Every addition names what the person gains; one that names nothing is a
   finding. A premise inherited from a Ticket, a record or an earlier read gets the same check.
7. **Progress, not proxy.** Count success by the person's progress — the job they came to do —
   not by a stand-in such as clicks, time spent or sign-ups.
8. **Decide for them.** Choose a sensible default the person can change, rather than adding a
   setting or a question.
9. **First value before commitment.** A newcomer gets a useful result before being asked to set
   up, sign up or configure anything.
10. **No surprises.** Before the person acts, they can tell what it costs, what happens to their
    data, who sees it, and whether it can be undone; prefer undo to a confirmation.
11. **Finish the core job before widening.** One job done completely beats several done partly.

Two more things the person meets at every step: each step shows what that step needs, with the
rest one step away; and an error says what happened and what to do next. Figures the person reads
are checked by the *Honest figures* probe in `tasks/UI.md`.
