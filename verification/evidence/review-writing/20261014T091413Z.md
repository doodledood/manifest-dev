### CRITICAL: the modal verb states the wrong obligation

> "Before you deploy the new release, you **may** rotate the signing key with `keys rotate --env prod`."

**What the rule requires:** a modal verb has to match the real obligation. The docs reference names this exact case as CRITICAL: "`may` where the action is required." "May" tells the reader the step is optional, or that they have permission to do it. The draft's next sentence says it isn't optional: skip it and every logged-in user is signed out.

**Why it fails:** a reader skimming the procedure reads "you may" as "you can skip this" and moves on. They only learn the cost from the next sentence, if they read it, and the cost is a production-wide sign-out. Following your team's rule produced this defect, so I'm reporting it even though the draft complies with the rule.

**Fix:** you don't need "must" or "required" to state a requirement. A plain imperative does it, keeps your rule, and keeps the friendly tone:

> Before you deploy the new release, rotate the signing key with `keys rotate --env prod`. If you skip this step, the release can't verify tokens signed by the old key, and every logged-in user is signed out.

(I also changed "this" to "this step" and added a comma before "and", since that joins two independent clauses. Both are minor.)

---

**About the rule itself (not a finding):** "always soften to *may*" will cause this same CRITICAL defect wherever a doc describes a step that's actually mandatory. You could amend the rule to "state requirements as plain imperatives." That keeps the tone friendly without implying a mandatory step is optional.

Nothing else reached MEDIUM. The heading is sentence case and imperative, the condition comes before the instruction, and the code reference is copyable.
